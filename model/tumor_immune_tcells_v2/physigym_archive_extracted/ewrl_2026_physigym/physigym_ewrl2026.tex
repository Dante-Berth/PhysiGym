\begin{abstract}
We present \physigym{}, a framework that exposes agent-based biological
simulations as standard reinforcement learning (RL) environments. It connects
the agent-based modelling framework \physicell{} to the Gymnasium API, so that
RL strategies can be tested on \emph{in silico} biological processes.
We illustrate it with a simplified tumor microenvironment (TME) toy model, in
which a Soft Actor-Critic agent learns targeted drug-delivery policies that move
the microenvironment toward an anti-tumoral state and, in the best episodes,
nearly eradicate the tumor.
We use the same case study to compare a family of observation spaces, from a
partially observed macrophage-count baseline, through spatial scalar summaries,
to multi-channel spatial images.
With training on a structured rectangle layout and testing on a held-out
network-field layout, image observations generalize markedly better than scalar
summaries. Image modes reach a mean held-out test return of
${\approx}{+}32$ to $+33$, spatial-scalar summaries train comparably ($+43$)
but fall to ${\approx}0$ out of distribution, and the macrophage-only POMDP
baseline does not learn ($-32$). When the two layouts are swapped, the gap
disappears: images protect the agent against overfitting a narrow training
distribution. The observation space, which \physigym{} makes configurable,
therefore determines policy quality and generalization in spatially structured
environments.
\physigym{} is open-source at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PhysiGym-76A7}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Biological systems are complex, dynamic, and often difficult to study in real
life.
In silico modeling aims to mimic these systems, providing a controlled
environment for exploring specific aspects of biological processes and testing
hypotheses.
Agent-based models (ABMs) are well suited to biological systems in which
agents (e.g.\ cells) interact according to predefined rules. They are used in
oncology and immunology to study emergent behaviors, and they can suggest novel
therapeutic strategies~\citep{johnson2023digitize,
laubenbacher2024toward, rocha2024multiscale}.

Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and agent-based models (ABMs) are widely
used to simulate the tumor microenvironment (TME). ODEs provide compact
system-level descriptions, while ABMs capture spatial and cellular
heterogeneity at higher computational cost. For dynamic treatment regimes
(DTRs)~\citep{gray1982arac}, however, simulation alone is insufficient:
designing adaptive, sequential interventions requires a control framework.
Reinforcement learning (RL), where an RL agent learns policies through
interaction with an environment, offers such a framework~\citep{barto1989learning}.
By modeling treatment regimes as a sequence of decisions, RL enables adaptive
control of biological simulations~\citep{chakraborty2014dynamic}. Efficient
experimentation and policy learning also require a standardized interface. We
therefore introduce \physigym{}, an interface that connects
\physicell{}~\citep{ghaffarizadeh2018physicell}, an ABM framework for
multicellular simulations of tissues or cell cultures (tumor microenvironment,
other disease tissues, or bacteria colonies), with
Gymnasium~\citep{towers2024gymnasium}, a widely adopted RL application
programming interface (API).
\physicell{} supports multiscale models of biological systems and couples them
to the BioFVM diffusion transport solver~\citep{ghaffarizadeh2016biofvm}.
Gymnasium provides a common structure for training and evaluating RL policies.

The first implementation of RL on an ABM framework based on BioFVM was proposed
by \citet{zade2020reinforcement}, which applied Q-learning~\citep{watkins1992q}
to optimize Temozolomide treatment regimes for patients with glioblastoma
multiforme.
More recently, \citet{aif2025failing} used \physicell{} as a complex
(high-fidelity) simulation environment for tumor growth, combined with a simple
(low-fidelity) RL training environment of coupled ordinary differential
equations (ODE) to learn treatment strategies for solid tumors.

\newpage
Both studies couple RL to a simulator for a \emph{single, fixed} problem. The
coupling is bespoke, the observation and action spaces are tied to the task, and
the code cannot be reused for another model. A practitioner who wants to ask a
different question (a new cell type, a different readout, an image observation in
place of a scalar one) must re-engineer the interface between simulator and RL
each time. \physigym{} addresses this complementary need. It is a
\emph{general, reusable} bridge that turns \emph{any} \physicell{} model into a
Gymnasium environment through subclassing and \physicell{}'s native user-parameter
and custom-data constructs (Section~\ref{sec:physigym}). Observation space,
action space, and reward become configuration choices that require no
re-implementation. The observation-space study in this paper depends on this
property: the same model is exposed in nine different ways with no modification
of the simulator.

\physigym{} bridges \physicell{} and Gymnasium to provide a reproducible and
scalable framework for integrating agent-based simulations of biological systems
with RL strategies. We present the motivation and design of \physigym{}, and
show how it connects ABMs and RL to control complex biological systems. We focus
on the tumor microenvironment, a biological system composed of cancer cells and
surrounding immune and stromal cells. Their interactions are known to determine
the response to immunotherapy, a therapeutic approach used in an increasing
number of cancer types.

To illustrate the framework, we build a simplified TME toy model and use it as
an end-to-end RL case study. The model also serves as a controlled testbed for
a question that arises in any spatially explicit biological environment: among
the observation spaces \physigym{} can expose (scalars, images, graphs), which
ones help an RL agent learn an effective dynamic treatment regime? Image-based
observations that preserve the spatial layout of cells and substrates outperform
compact scalar summaries, both in cumulative return and in generalization to
held-out spatial configurations. This finding concerns one deliberately
simplified model. The primary contribution of this work is \physigym{}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~\physigym{}: an open-source framework that bridges the \physicell{} ABM and
the Gymnasium RL API, turning any \physicell{} model into an RL environment with
minimal code changes.
(2)~A simplified TME toy model demonstrating an end-to-end RL case study in
which an agent learns a spatial drug-delivery policy that drives the
microenvironment toward an anti-tumoral state.
(3)~A systematic comparison of observation spaces on the toy model, showing
that spatially explicit (image) representations help RL learn and generalize
better than scalar summaries.

\subsection{\physigym{} design and implementation}
\label{sec:physigym}

\physicell{} is an open-source ABM framework written in C++ that models
multicellular systems using classical mechanics and the BioFVM diffusive
transport solver~\citep{ghaffarizadeh2018physicell, ghaffarizadeh2016biofvm}.
Cells are agents governed by type-specific rule sets; substrates such as oxygen
or cytokines diffuse through the domain. Intracellular signalling
networks~\citep{letort2018physiboss, ponce2023physiboss, ruscone2024physiboss}
and extracellular matrix fibres~\citep{noel2024physimess} can be added, yielding
ABMs that are spatially explicit in 2D or 3D, off-lattice, and
multiscale~\citep{metzcar2019review}.

RL frames sequential decision-making as a Markov Decision Process
(MDP)~\citep{bellman1957markovian, puterman2014markov} with state space $S$,
action space $A$, transition model $T$, and reward $R$.
The objective is
\[
  \underset{\pi\in\Pi}{\arg\max}\;
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau}\gamma^t r_t \mid s_0,\,\pi\right].
\]
In our setting the agent never sees the full simulator state (every cell's
position and internal variables, every voxel's substrate concentrations); it
receives an observation $o_t = f(s_t)$ from an observation space $O$ chosen by the
user, so the control problem is formally a partially observed MDP (POMDP). The
observation map $f$ is the component we vary in Section~\ref{sec:obs}.
Gymnasium~\citep{towers2024gymnasium} is the standard Python interface for MDPs,
and it promotes modularity and code reuse across algorithms and environments.

\physigym{} connects these two ecosystems by extending the Python
interpreter~\citep{rossum1995extending, psf2024extending} with \physicell{}.
The C++ simulation loop is split into three Python-callable functions:
\texttt{physicell\_start}, \texttt{physicell\_step}, and
\texttt{physicell\_stop}. For most models only \texttt{physicell\_step} needs
to be modified (e.g.\ to inject a drug). Three read functions,
\texttt{get\_cell}, \texttt{get\_microenv}, and \texttt{get\_graph}, return
cell positions, substrate concentration fields, and cell neighbourhood graphs
respectively. All are implemented in \texttt{physicellmodule.cpp}.

The interface between \physicell{} and Gymnasium uses \physicell{}'s built-in
\emph{user parameter} and \emph{custom data} constructs, configurable through
PhysiCell Studio~\citep{heiland2024physicellstudio} without recompilation.
Users subclass \texttt{CorePhysiCellEnv} in \texttt{physicell\_model.py} to
specify observation space, action space, and reward; the base class handles
all model-independent Gymnasium bookkeeping.
Any existing \physicell{} model can therefore become a \physigym{} environment
with minimal code changes.
\physigym{} is tested on Linux, WSL, and macOS, with continuous integration via
GitHub Actions. Documentation and tutorials are at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PhysiGym-76A7/man}.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows the resulting dataflow.
Listing~\ref{lst:env} gives a complete minimal environment, in which the user
overrides only the observation, action, and reward, and the base class handles
the \physicell{} start/step/stop loop.

\subsection{Implementation of a Tumor Microenvironment Toy Model}
\label{sec:model}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We developed a simplified tumor microenvironment (TME) \emph{toy model} to
illustrate the potential uses of \physigym{}. The model is deliberately small
and computationally cheap, so that RL agents can be trained to convergence many
times (across observation spaces and seeds). It keeps the spatial,
multicellular, and chemically coupled structure that distinguishes agent-based
models (ABMs) from ODE surrogates. It includes three cell types, five diffusible
substrates, and a set of simple agent-based rules. The agent controls tumor
progression by modulating the whole microenvironment, and it has no means of
killing cancer cells directly.

\paragraph{Domain and Dynamics.}
The continuous-space environment is simulated on a $64 \times 64\,\mu\text{m}$ 2D domain. 
The RL gym step interval is $\Delta t = 15$\,min, with episodes running up to 
$10{,}080$\,min (7 simulated days).

\paragraph{Cell populations and control problem.}
The control problem is highly non-linear and indirect.
The agent's action (deploying a localized therapeutic, \texttt{drug\_1}) has
zero direct cytotoxicity. The agent must learn to modify the transition dynamics
of the environment by reversing a spatial immunosuppression loop. Three
interacting cell populations move along biochemical gradients (chemotaxis):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tumor cells (target):} immobile cells that divide continuously.
    They secrete a chemical signal (\texttt{tumor\_molecule}) that suppresses
    the local immune response.

    \item \textbf{Macrophages (mediators):} mobile, immortal cells that migrate
    toward the tumor. Their behavior depends on their state: the tumor's
    chemical signal converts them from an anti-tumoral to a pro-tumoral state.

    \item \textbf{T-cells (effectors):} mobile, immortal cells that can trigger
    tumor apoptosis. Pro-tumoral macrophages strongly repel them.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is a simplified abstraction of \emph{macrophage-repolarising
immunotherapy}. Tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) with a pro-tumoral,
M2-like phenotype are abundant in many solid tumours and are associated with
poor prognosis~\citep{mantovani2017tumour}; they can physically exclude CD8$^+$
T-cells from tumour nests and thereby limit checkpoint-blockade
efficacy~\citep{peranzoni2018macrophages}. Re-educating TAMs toward an
anti-tumoral, M1-like state, for example with CSF-1R
inhibition~\citep{pyonteck2013csf} or TLR7/8 agonists delivered by
nanoparticles that accumulate in TAMs~\citep{rodell2018tlr7}, is an active
therapeutic strategy. \texttt{drug\_1} plays the role of such a
TAM-repolarising agent: a locally delivered immunomodulator with no direct
cytotoxicity. We use this mechanism qualitatively; the rates in the toy model are
not fit to any of these studies (see Limitations).

The result is a self-reinforcing feedback loop: the tumor protects itself by
converting macrophages, which then block T-cells from reaching it. As
illustrated in Figures~\ref{fig:mechanism} and~\ref{fig:tme_network}, the agent
maximizes reward by learning where to deploy the drug so that it re-programs
macrophages to an anti-tumoral state. This intervention modifies the chemical
gradients and opens a path for T-cells to reach the tumor and kill it.
\texttt{drug\_1} does not diffuse: it acts only on the disc where the agent
injects it, and the largest disc the action space permits covers $6.3\%$ of
the domain. The repolarised macrophages emit a diffusing anti-tumoral factor,
which forms the gradient the T-cells follow. Where the drug is placed therefore
decides which macrophages are repolarised, and the agent must aim.

\subsection{Implementation of an RL approach to control the TME}
\label{sec:rl}

Our objective is complete tumor eradication with minimal drug exposure. We
describe the action space $A$, the reward $R$, and the training and test
distributions. The observation space $S$ is the component we vary across
experiments. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares a family of observation modes,
from a partially observed scalar baseline through spatial scalar summaries to
multi-channel spatial images, to assess whether spatially explicit
representations help the agent learn and generalize on this toy model.

We train every configuration with Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC)~\citep{haarnoja2018soft}, using an asynchronous parallel implementation
(Appendix~\ref{sec:training}); the network architectures and hyperparameters,
including how they were selected, are given in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
Each run uses $13$ parallel environments. Every fourth episode of each environment
is a \emph{test} episode: its initial condition is freshly sampled from the
held-out network-field distribution, and the agent's actions do not enter the
replay buffer. All other episodes are training episodes on freshly sampled
rectangle layouts. A run therefore collects on the order of $200$ held-out test
episodes, each on a distinct initial condition, and the per-run test return we
report is the exponentially weighted mean (span $50$) over the last test
episodes at the end of training. Each observation mode is trained with $n=5$
independent training seeds, and all uncertainty statements aggregate over these
seeds (bootstrap CIs, Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}; significance tests,
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Action space.}
At each gym step the agent selects
$a_t = (\text{dose},\,x,\,y,\,r)\in[0,1]^4$,
where dose is the drug concentration applied, $(x,y)$ is the injection centre
(normalised domain coordinates), and $r$ is the injection radius (normalised
by the domain diagonal).

\paragraph{Reward.}
The reward is a normalised tumor-suppression signal minus a weighted drug-cost penalty:
\begin{equation}
  r_t \;=\; w_{\text{cell}}\,
  \frac{c_{t-1} - c_t}{\,c_{t-1}(e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1)\,}
  \;-\; w_{\text{dose}}\, d_t,
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $c_t$ is the alive tumor-cell count at step $t$,
$\lambda$ is the intrinsic growth rate (from \texttt{PhysiCell\_settings.xml}),
$d_t$ is the normalised dose spent, $w_{\text{cell}}=0.3$, and $w_{\text{dose}}=2$.
The denominator $c_{t-1}(e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1)$ is the number of new tumor cells
expected over one step from unchecked intrinsic exponential growth, so the ratio
measures the observed change \emph{relative to} the growth the agent had to
overcome. This makes the tumor term scale-free in the absolute cell count and
comparable across initial conditions with different tumor sizes: a value of $+1$
means one step's worth of expected growth was fully cancelled, independent of how
many cells were present.
Only the ratio $w_{\text{dose}}/w_{\text{cell}}\approx6.7$ affects which policy
is optimal. It makes a unit of dose expensive against a unit of normalised tumor
control, so blanket dosing has to justify its cost.

\subsubsection*{Training and Test Distributions}
\label{sec:distributions}

We train and test on two \emph{structurally distinct} spatial distributions, so that test performance measures out-of-distribution (OOD) generalisation to an unseen geometry:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rectangle (training)}: Each cell type is placed uniformly in a narrow vertical strip, with non-overlapping strip positions (Appendix~\ref{app:ic}). This is a highly structured, low-entropy layout.
    \item \textbf{Network-field (held-out test)}: Initial positions are sampled from a spatially correlated random field (Appendix~\ref{app:ic}), producing irregular, clustered configurations never seen during training.
\end{itemize}
The transfer from the simple, regular rectangle to the richer, irregular network-field is demanding. A policy that exploits the fixed strip geometry of the rectangle cannot solve the network-field, so the test return measures whether an observation space encodes \emph{transferable} structure. Figure~\ref{fig:ic_distributions} shows representative examples of both distributions.

\subsection{Observation space $O$: modes compared}
\label{sec:obs}

The simulator can expose several kinds of data: some carry explicit spatial
information, others are spaceless summaries (e.g.\ a small vector of global
statistics). Because \physigym{} can build all of these from the same
simulation, we use the toy model to study how the choice of observation space
affects the dynamic treatment regime that RL learns. We compare nine modes, from
a partially observed scalar baseline to multi-channel spatial images, on
cumulative return and on generalization to unseen spatial configurations.
Figure~\ref{fig:obs_modes} shows one simulator state as seen by three of them.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/fig_obs_modes}
  \caption{One held-out network-field initial condition seen by three
    observation modes. \textbf{(a)} Simulator state: every cell's position and
    type. \textbf{(b)} \texttt{scalars\_macrophages}: four counts, with no
    position. At $t=0$ both polarising fields are still empty, so the M1/M2
    rule labels every macrophage M1. \textbf{(c)} Spatial-scalar summary with
    $k=1$: a centroid ($\times$) and a spread per type. The positions in grey
    are discarded. The macrophages form several clumps, and their centroid falls
    in empty space. \textbf{(d)} \texttt{img\_mc\_cells}: three $64{\times}64$
    occupancy channels, which keep every position.}
  \label{fig:obs_modes}
\end{figure}
Let $N_c=3$ (tumor, T-cell, macrophage) and $N_s=5$ substrates.
Several modes optionally split the macrophage population into its
\emph{M1} (anti-tumoral) and \emph{M2} (pro-tumoral) polarisation states, which
we denote with the suffix \texttt{m1m2}; the classification rule is given below.

\paragraph{Macrophage polarisation (M1/M2).}
Macrophages do not carry an explicit polarisation label in the simulator;
their state is implicit in the local chemical environment. We recover it from
the microenvironment: for each alive macrophage we look up the two competing
substrate fields at its nearest voxel and classify it as \emph{M2}
(pro-tumoral) if $\mathrm{conc}_{\texttt{pro\_tumoral}} >
\mathrm{conc}_{\texttt{anti\_tumoral}}$ at that voxel, and \emph{M1}
(anti-tumoral) otherwise. The pro-/anti-tumoral balance, which is the quantity
the agent must reverse, thus becomes observable as a count (\texttt{scalars}
modes) or as two spatial channels (\texttt{img} modes). Without this step it
would remain latent in the substrate fields.

\subsubsection*{Scalar Observation Spaces}

\paragraph{\texttt{scalars\_macrophages} (POMDP baseline):}
Alive count per cell type, with the macrophage slot split into M1 and M2 counts;
each is normalised to the initial population ($s = c/c_{\text{init}} - 1$).
This mode exposes only \emph{how many} macrophages are polarised each way. It
contains no spatial information: neither cell positions nor substrate maps.
It is therefore a strongly partially observed (POMDP) baseline. The state
variables that govern the transition dynamics (positions of cells and gradients)
are hidden, and the agent acts on a heavily compressed summary.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{N_c + 1}$

\paragraph{\texttt{spatial\_scalars\_cells\_substrates}:}
Per-type normalised cell counts and per-substrate maxima, augmented with $6$
$k$-means spatial statistics per cell type (cluster occupancy and centroids),
giving a compact spatial summary without full images.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{N_c + N_s + 6kN_c}$

\paragraph{\texttt{spatial\_scalars\_cells\_m1m2} / \texttt{...\_substrates\_m1m2}:}
As above but with the macrophage population split into M1/M2 for both the
scalar counts and the $k$-means spatial features
(\texttt{...\_substrates\_m1m2} additionally keeps the substrate maxima).
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{(N_c+1) + [N_s] + 6k(N_c+1)}$

\paragraph{\texttt{spatial\_scalars\_cells\_spatial\_no\_scalars\_substrates\_m1m2}:}
M1/M2 cell counts plus $k$-means spatial features for \emph{both} cell types
and substrate fields (spatially resolved substrate summaries in place of scalar
maxima).
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{(N_c+1) + 6kN_s + 6k(N_c+1)}$

\subsubsection*{Image-Based Observation Spaces}

\paragraph{\texttt{img\_mc\_cells} (I1):}
One $64{\times}64$ channel per cell type.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{N_c \times 64 \times 64}$ (\texttt{uint8})

\paragraph{\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_m1m2}:}
I1 with the macrophage channel split into separate M1 and M2 spatial maps.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{(N_c+1) \times 64 \times 64}$ (\texttt{uint8})

\paragraph{\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_substrates} (I2):}
I1 concatenated with all five substrate concentration maps, providing direct
spatial feedback on drug distribution and the polarising factors.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{(N_c + N_s) \times 64 \times 64}$ (\texttt{uint8})

\paragraph{\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_substrates\_m1m2} (full information):}
I2 with the additional M1/M2 macrophage channels. This mode carries every
signal the others expose (cell positions, substrate fields, and the explicit
polarisation split) and serves as the near-fully-observed reference for the
compressed modes.
\\ \textit{Space:} $\mathbb{R}^{(N_c + N_s + 2) \times 64 \times 64}$ (\texttt{uint8})

\subsubsection*{An observability spectrum}

Together these modes form an observability spectrum, from
\texttt{scalars\_macrophages}, a POMDP that sees only polarisation counts, to
\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_substrates\_m1m2}, which spatially resolves every state
variable relevant to the control problem. The indirect drug mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:model}) requires multi-step credit assignment: the agent
injects drug, macrophages repolarise over several steps, anti-tumoral gradients
shift, T-cells migrate, and only then do tumor cells die. Scalar summaries
cannot tell the agent whether an injection reached macrophages, whether
polarisation occurred, or where T-cells will concentrate next. The per-pixel
substrate and M1/M2 channels of the image modes show the polarisation state and
the drug distribution in space at each step.

\subsection{Key findings}

\paragraph{Separation under distribution shift.}
On the training rectangle layout the spatial-scalar summary S3s is fully
competitive: its training return ($+42.9$) is on par with the best image mode
I2 ($+45.3$). The modes separate only out of distribution. On the held-out
network-field, the three image modes retain ${\approx}{+}32$ to $+33$ test
return, while S3s falls to $-0.0$. Ranked on training fit alone, the scalar
summary would appear among the best modes. The OOD test shows that it has
overfit the fixed strip geometry of the rectangle. This separation is
statistically supported: the image modes' $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals on the mean
test return lie entirely above $+23$ and do not overlap those of any
spatial-scalar mode, which all reach down to $+0.4$ or below
(Table~\ref{tab:results}; all nine modes in Table~\ref{tab:allmodes}).
Pooling the per-seed test returns of the three image modes against the four
spatial-scalar modes, the image family is higher with a Mann--Whitney
$U$-test $p=6\times10^{-7}$ and a Cliff's $\delta=+0.97$ (near-complete
rank separation), so the gap is not an artefact of seed selection
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{Transfer of image observations.}
All three image modes, \texttt{img\_mc\_cells} (I1, $+33.4$),
\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_substrates} (I2, $+32.7$), and the full-information
\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_substrates\_m1m2} (I2m, $+32.5$), generalize to the
network-field, and their confidence intervals overlap.
Since I1 already generalizes well, preserving the \emph{spatial layout} of cells
is the primary driver of transfer in this toy model. The extra substrate and
M1/M2 channels of I2/I2m do not further separate on average, though I2m gives
the single best test return.

\paragraph{POMDP baseline and random policy.}
\texttt{scalars\_macrophages}, which observes only M1/M2 counts and no spatial
information, does not improve on either distribution (train $-20.4$, test
$-31.8$) and does \emph{not} beat a random-action policy
(train $-18.4$, test $-25.3$; RAND in Table~\ref{tab:results}). Its test return
is, if anything, below random, and the two bootstrap CIs do not overlap
($[-33.4,-29.4]$ vs.\ $[-28.2,-22.8]$, Table~\ref{tab:results}). The
macrophage-count signal is therefore misleading as well as uninformative: it
steers dosing worse than chance.
Knowing \emph{how many} macrophages are polarised without knowing \emph{where}
the drug, cells, and gradients are is insufficient to solve the indirect,
spatially mediated control problem. The choice of observation space can thus
make an otherwise learnable task effectively unlearnable. Every spatial-scalar
mode, although it does not generalize, still exceeds the random baseline on test
($\ge 0$ vs.\ $-25.3$), so these modes learn \emph{some} transferable
structure, far less than the image modes.


\subsection{The role of the M1/M2 split and spatial scalars}

The full mode family (Appendix~\ref{app:allmodes}, Table~\ref{tab:allmodes})
lets us isolate the effect of the explicit M1/M2 polarisation split. Adding it
to the cell image (I1$\to$\texttt{img\_mc\_cells\_m1m2}) does not improve
average OOD test return ($+33.4\to+26.0$; the difference is not significant,
Welch $p=0.40$ at $n=5$), and adding it on top of the full image
(I2$\to$I2m) leaves it essentially unchanged ($+32.7\to+32.5$). With only five
seeds, an equivalence test (TOST, margin $\pm10\approx1\sigma$) does not confirm
equivalence for either pair. The data therefore show \emph{no detectable
improvement} from the pre-computed split on this toy model, and do not prove a
null effect (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). A plausible mechanism is that, once the
substrate fields are spatially available, the CNN can already read polarisation
from the \texttt{pro\_tumoral}/\texttt{anti\_tumoral} channels. None of the
spatial-scalar modes generalize (all within $[-0.0,+9.5]$ test return), whether
they carry the M1/M2 split or spatially resolved substrate summaries. Their
common failure is the loss of per-cell spatial layout, which the $k$-means
summaries do not preserve.

Appendix~\ref{app:episodes} shows a matched example episode comparing an image
mode, a spatial-scalar mode, and the POMDP baseline rolled out from an identical
network-field initial condition. Without a spatially explicit representation,
the agent cannot target the drug precisely enough to convert dose into
macrophage repolarisation on an unseen layout. The image mode reaches
near-eradication ($3$ tumor cells), while the scalar and POMDP modes leave $70$
and $130$ cells respectively.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The central outcome of this work is \physigym{}: a framework that lets RL agents
control a full agent-based TME through a standardised interface, without
recompilation, and with arbitrary observation and action spaces. The toy-model
case study shows the framework end-to-end. An agent learns a spatial
drug-delivery policy that moves the microenvironment toward an anti-tumoral
state and, in the best episodes, nearly eradicates the tumor
($3$ of $124$ tumor cells left in the matched held-out episode of
Appendix~\ref{app:episodes}).

The toy model also gives a clear result on observation spaces, which appears
only under distribution shift. On the training rectangle layout, spatial-scalar
summaries are fully competitive with image observations. On the held-out
network-field, image observations generalize to ${\approx}{+}33$ test return
while the scalar summaries fall to ${\approx}0$. The CNN in the image modes can
compute cross-channel co-localisation statistics (e.g.\ whether \texttt{drug\_1}
and macrophages overlap at the same location) directly from the pixel layout,
and these statistics transfer across geometries. Scalar modes that encode
$k$-means cluster centroids and absolute positions overfit the fixed strip
geometry of the training layout. Once cell layout is spatially
available, preserving the substrate fields (I2) or pre-computing the M1/M2 split
(I2m) does not further improve average transfer over the cell-only image (I1) on
this toy model, though the full-information mode gives the single best test
return.

\paragraph{Drug aiming under geometry shift.}
The overfit is visible in the actions of the policies. For every logged episode we compute the mean distance
between the drug-injection centre (\texttt{action\_x}, \texttt{action\_y}) and
the tumour centroid (from the episode initial conditions), averaged over the
steps on which a dose is actually applied, and compare this aiming error between
the training rectangle and the held-out network-field
(Fig.~\ref{fig:aiming_transfer}). On the training layout all six modes aim
equally well (mean normalised error ${\approx}0.34$--$0.36$ for both families).
Under the geometry shift, image modes hold their aim or slightly improve it
(I2: $0.359\!\to\!0.343$; I2m: $0.352\!\to\!0.333$), whereas the aim of every
scalar mode \emph{degrades} (S3s: $0.341\!\to\!0.414$; S3m: $0.348\!\to\!0.406$)
and its step-to-step variance roughly doubles. The scalar policies have learned
\emph{where} the rectangle tumour usually lies, and not \emph{how to locate} the
tumour. When the network-field moves it, they inject into empty tissue, and the
median final tumour count on test rises accordingly (image ${\approx}73$--$99$
vs.\ scalar $107$--$136$). The aggregate return gap thus results from an aiming
failure induced by position-absolute features: a centroid that lies between
clumps of cells points at empty tissue (Figure~\ref{fig:obs_modes}c).

  \caption{Drug-aiming alignment under geometry shift. Bars show the mean
    normalised distance between the drug-injection centre and the tumour
    centroid over dosed steps (lower is better aiming); error bars are the
    across-episode standard deviation. On the training rectangle (blue) image
    and scalar observations aim equally well. On the held-out network-field
    (red) image modes retain their aim while every scalar mode degrades with

  \label{fig:aiming_transfer}

\paragraph{Reverse direction.}
\label{sec:reverse}
To check that the network-field is not simply an easier test layout, we repeated
the study with the roles swapped (train on network-field, test on rectangle) and
compared image and scalar modes in matched pairs. In the original direction
the image mode has the higher held-out return in $11/13$ pairs (mean
$\Delta=+27.1$, 95\% CI $[+16.3,+36.2]$, sign test $p=0.01$), and a smaller
generalisation gap in $13/13$ ($\Delta=-34.5$, $p=10^{-4}$). In the reverse
direction the return advantage disappears ($7/15$ pairs, $\Delta=-6.5$,
$[-16.9,+4.2]$, $p=0.70$), and the scalar modes also keep their drug aim under
the shift (aiming degradation $+0.008$ against $+0.049$ in the forward direction).
Image modes still fit the critic better in both directions (TD residual lower in
$13/13$ and $15/15$ pairs). Our interpretation is that scalar agents trained on
the irregular network-field cannot memorise a fixed strip position and must use
their spatial features, which then transfer. Spatially explicit observations
therefore make the agent robust when the training distribution is narrow and
structured, a frequent situation in practice (few, stereotyped training
geometries). With an irregular training distribution, they give no return
advantage in this study.

These observations concern one deliberately simplified model of biological
control. In more complex or realistic models, or with different reward designs,
action spaces, or RL algorithms, the relative value of cell versus substrate,
and of scalar versus image, may differ. \physigym{} is designed to make such
questions answerable in a reproducible way.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The toy model is intentionally small (64\,$\mu$m, 2D) and cheap to simulate.
Scaling to 3D or larger domains will increase the cost of image observations.
All experiments use a single RL algorithm (SAC) with one network size per
observation family and one reward setting. Alternative algorithms, model-based
methods, other reward weightings, or larger encoders may interact differently
with observation spaces, and we have not tested whether the image advantage
survives these variations.
Each mode is trained with a modest, fixed number of seeds ($n=5$). We
report bootstrap confidence intervals on every mean (Table~\ref{tab:results},
Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}), but more seeds would strengthen the conclusions.
The image advantage is specific to training on a narrow layout
(Section~\ref{sec:reverse}). We test only two layouts, and a wider family of
train/test distributions would show where between these two extremes it appears.
The model's rules and parameters (growth, secretion, chemotaxis, and
polarisation rates) are chosen to be biologically plausible and to reproduce the
qualitative immunosuppression loop of Section~\ref{sec:model}. They are
\emph{illustrative and not fit to a specific tumour or dataset}. We make no
quantitative claim about a real cancer, and we have not performed a sensitivity
analysis over these parameters. Grounding the rules in measured rates, and
verifying that the observation-space conclusion survives this grounding, is
important future work. \physigym{} is designed to make such studies routine,
since the calibration resides in the \physicell{} configuration and not in the
RL code. Complex biological models and 3D models are left to future work.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented \physigym{}, an open-source framework that bridges the
\physicell{} ABM with Gymnasium RL, enabling direct policy learning in
spatially explicit biological simulations.
Using a TME model with an indirect drug mechanism, we showed that spatially
explicit image observations are decisive for \emph{generalization}. With
training on a structured rectangle layout and testing on a held-out
network-field, image modes retain ${\approx}{+}32$ to $+33$ test return, while
spatial-scalar summaries, competitive during training, fall to ${\approx}0$ out
of distribution, and a macrophage-count-only POMDP baseline does not learn.
The gap concerns generalization and depends on the training distribution: with
the layouts swapped, scalar summaries generalize as well as images. Spatially
explicit observations therefore protect against overfitting a narrow training
distribution.
\physigym{} provides the infrastructure to study such questions reproducibly,
and we expect it to serve as a testbed for the computational biology and RL
communities.

\section{Generation of Initial Conditions}
\label{app:ic}

\paragraph{Rectangle (training layout).}
Each cell type is placed uniformly in a vertical strip of width $10\%$ of the
$x$-axis, with non-overlapping strip positions sampled from $[0.1,0.2]$,
$[0.3,0.5]$, $[0.6,0.8]$ (shuffled across types). This is a highly regular,
low-entropy layout.

\paragraph{Network-field (held-out test layout).}
White Gaussian noise $\eta(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is convolved
with a Gaussian kernel $G_\sigma$ ($\sigma=\ell_c/\sqrt{2}$,
$\ell_c=45$\,µm) to produce a correlated field $F$.
An additional local noise component $\tilde\xi$ filtered at width
$\ell_c/3$ is added with small weight $\alpha$ to introduce local
heterogeneity.
The field is normalised to $[0,1]$ and cells are sampled from positions
with $\hat{F}>0.55$, with probability proportional to $\hat{F}$, producing
irregular, clustered configurations that are structurally distinct from the
training rectangle.

\section{Training algorithm and setup}
\label{sec:training}

For our control objective (maximal cancer-cell eradication with minimal drug
administration) we use Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC)~\citep{haarnoja2018soft}, a state-of-the-art RL algorithm for continuous
control. SAC is well suited to our setting due to its high sample efficiency,
and as a deep RL method it accommodates different observation types, such as
image-based representations, through Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs)~\citep{o2015introduction}. The simulator dominates the computational
cost, and we therefore adopt an asynchronous parallel
training framework~\citep{mnih2016asynchronous, liu2025asynchronous}.
A GPU-resident learner updates policy and value networks asynchronously while
$13$ CPU-resident environment processes generate experience in parallel.

\paragraph{Compute.}
Each run trains a single observation mode for one seed to $10^5$ agent decisions
($6\!\times\!10^5$ simulator steps) in roughly $3$--$5$ hours of wall-clock time
(including simulation, experience collection, and learner updates)
on a single workstation (one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU-resident learner with
$13$ CPU-resident environment workers on an Intel Core i9-13900K, 32 logical
cores). The asynchronous design decouples the GPU learner from the
comparatively slow \physicell{} simulator, so throughput is limited by the CPU
environment workers and not by gradient updates. This makes the full
observation-space sweep (nine modes, $5$ seeds each) tractable on a single
machine.

\paragraph{Bridge overhead and throughput.}
To characterise the cost of the \physigym{} bridge, we time a single
environment's \texttt{step()} in isolation (median over $60$ steps after warm-up,
one worker, on the workstation above). Each \texttt{step()} runs the C++
\physicell{} simulator step and the Python observation read-back that the bridge
adds (Table~\ref{tab:throughput}). A single environment sustains
${\approx}12$--$13$ steps/s. Switching from the scalar POMDP observation to a
full nine-channel image observation raises the per-step time by only
${\approx}13\%$ ($76.9\!\to\!86.3$\,ms), and the cell-only image is
indistinguishable from the scalar mode. The observation read-back is a small
fraction of the step, which the simulator dominates. The choice of observation
space (Section~\ref{sec:obs}) therefore has little effect on wall-clock time,
and aggregate throughput scales with the number of parallel CPU workers, not
with observation richness. The $13$-worker configuration reaches on the
order of ${\approx}150$\,steps/s of aggregate experience, so the
$6\!\times\!10^5$-step budget plus learner updates completes in the
$3$--$5$\,h per run reported above.

\paragraph{Action repeat.}
Each action selected by the agent is held fixed for $6$ consecutive simulator
steps (\emph{action repeat} of $6$) before the next decision. The controlled
process is slow and indirect (drug injection $\to$ macrophage repolarisation
$\to$ gradient shift $\to$ T-cell migration $\to$ tumor death), and a fresh
decision at every $15$-minute step injects jitter into the drug field without
changing the dynamics. The $10^5$ agent decisions per run therefore correspond
to $6\!\times\!10^5$ simulator steps, the unit of all step axes in
Figures~\ref{fig:train_curves}--\ref{fig:test_curves}. The value AR$=6$ comes
from a sensitivity sweep (Appendix~\ref{app:actionrepeat}).

\subsection{Asynchronous SAC}
\label{app:async_sac}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Asynchronous SAC with Parallel Environments~\citep{liu2025asynchronous}}
\label{alg:async_sac}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Config $d_\text{arg}$, buffer size $|\mathcal{B}|$, LRs, $\gamma$, $\tau$
\State Init shared queues \texttt{actor\_queue}, \texttt{sample\_queue};
       stop signal \texttt{stop\_event}
\State \textbf{Actor Process (CPU):} init $N_\text{env}=13$ envs, local $\pi_{\theta_a}$
\While{not \texttt{stop\_event}}
  \State Receive $\theta$ from \texttt{actor\_queue} if available
  \State Act: random if $t\!<\!T_\text{start}$, else $\pi_{\theta_a}$
  \State Step envs; push $(s,a,r,s',d)$ to \texttt{sample\_queue}
\EndWhile
\State \textbf{Learner Process (GPU):} init $\pi_\theta$, $Q_{\phi_{1,2}}$, targets, $\mathcal{D}$
\While{total samples $< T$}
  \State Drain \texttt{sample\_queue} into $\mathcal{D}$
  \For{$N_\text{loops}$ gradient steps}
    \State Sample minibatch; compute SAC target $y$; update $Q$, $\pi$, $\alpha$;
           soft-update targets
  \EndFor
  \State Push updated $\theta$ to \texttt{actor\_queue}
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\[

\end{align*}

\paragraph{Hyperparameter selection.}
We tuned the environment-side hyperparameters, not the SAC optimiser, and did so
once, before and independently of the observation-space comparison. A Latin
hypercube design of $20$ configurations over the action repeat and the maximum
per-step change of each targeted action component ($\Delta x$, $\Delta y$,
$\Delta r$) was evaluated with probe rollouts ($6$ seeds $\times$ $2$ episodes
per configuration) and scored by a standardised composite of tumour reduction,
total dose, and action smoothness. This selected action repeat $6$ and
$(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta r)\approx(0.25,0.25,0.03)$. The composite does not
depend on the reward weights, so the search gives no information about them.
The weights $w_{\text{cell}}=0.3$ and $w_{\text{dose}}=2$ were set by the
argument on their ratio in Section~\ref{sec:rl}. The SAC hyperparameters in
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} are standard values, shared by every observation mode
and not tuned per mode, so no mode benefits from a larger tuning budget than
another.

\subsection{Choice of action repeat}
\label{app:actionrepeat}

We select the action-repeat value with a dedicated sensitivity sweep, run
independently of the main observation-space experiments. We sweep
action\_repeat $\in\{1,\dots,8\}$ and, for each value, measure three end-of-run
quantities aggregated over repeated rollouts (with $95\%$ bootstrap CIs):
tumor reduction (control efficacy), an action-smoothness cost
$\sum_t\|a_t-a_{t-1}\|^2$ (policy jitter; lower is better), and a composite
objective combining tumor reduction, dose, and smoothness
(Figure~\ref{fig:action_repeat}).

Two effects drive the choice. (i)~\emph{Smoothness}: the action-smoothness cost
falls steeply and monotonically with action repeat, from ${\approx}110$ at
AR$=1$ to ${\approx}17$ at AR$=6$, and then flattens, so most of the
stabilisation benefit is already captured by AR$=6$ and larger values add little.
This matches the physical timescale of the task: with a $15$-minute decision
interval on a slow immune process, re-deciding every simulator step injects
high-frequency drug-field jitter that does not change the underlying dynamics.
(ii)~\emph{Composite objective}: the objective is worst at AR$=1$
($-0.88$) and rises to a plateau over AR$=5$--$8$, with AR$=6$ at
$-0.07$. Within that plateau the values lie within each other's confidence
intervals, and AR$=6$ is chosen as the smallest repeat that reaches the
objective plateau while sitting at the knee of the smoothness curve.

\paragraph{Bootstrap confidence intervals.}
\label{app:bootstrap}
To quantify uncertainty on the \emph{mean} return with $n=5$ seeds, we resample the seeds of each
mode with replacement $B=10{,}000$ times, recompute the mean end-of-training
return of each resample, and report the $2.5$th--$97.5$th percentiles as a
$95\%$ confidence interval. The image modes' test CIs lie entirely above $+23$,
whereas every spatial-scalar CI \emph{contains zero} (e.g.\ S3s
$[-6.2,+9.1]$) or nearly so. The POMDP baseline's CI $[-33.4,-29.4]$ lies
entirely below that of the random policy, $[-28.2,-22.8]$.

\section{Significance tests and effect sizes}
\label{app:stats}

The bootstrap CIs (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}) quantify uncertainty on each
mode's mean; here we add explicit tests so the main claims do not rest on visual
CI overlap alone. All tests use the same per-seed end-of-training EWMA-50 test
returns ($n=5$ per mode) and introduce no new runs.

\paragraph{Image vs.\ spatial-scalar generalization.}
Pooling the three image modes' per-seed test returns ($n=15$, mean $+32.8$)
against the four spatial-scalar modes' ($n=20$, mean $+3.7$), a one-sided
Mann--Whitney $U$-test gives $U=296$, $p=6.2\times10^{-7}$, and Cliff's
$\delta=+0.97$, i.e.\ near-complete rank separation between the families. This is the
formal counterpart to the disjoint CIs in Table~\ref{tab:allmodes}.

\paragraph{M1/M2-split ablation and equivalence test.}
For the two nested pairs we report both the difference-of-means test and a
two-one-sided-tests (TOST) equivalence test against a margin of $\pm10$
(${\approx}1\sigma$ of a single mode's seed spread). I1 vs.\ I1m:
$+33.4$ vs.\ $+26.0$, difference not significant (Welch $p=0.40$) but TOST does
\emph{not} establish equivalence ($p=0.38$). I2 vs.\ I2m: $+32.7$ vs.\ $+32.5$,
Welch $p=0.97$, TOST $p=0.05$ (borderline). At $n=5$ the data therefore support
``no detectable improvement from the pre-computed split'' but do not license the
stronger claim of statistical equivalence; more seeds would be needed to settle it.

The numbers above are produced by \texttt{figures\_plotting/stats\_tests.py},
which reads the same cached per-seed curves as the tables and figures.



\section{Example Episode Comparisons}
\label{app:episodes}

Figure~\ref{fig:episode} shows a single matched test episode comparing an image
mode (\Itwo{}), a spatial-scalar mode (\Sthree{}), and the POMDP baseline
(\Sone{}) step by step. For an exact comparison, all three agents are rolled
out (deterministically, using the policy mean action) from the
\emph{identical} held-out network-field initial condition, the same
$124$-tumor-cell layout replayed from file. The observation mode is therefore
the only difference between the three trajectories.

The three regimes separate clearly in this episode. \Itwo{} drives the tumor to
near-eradication ($3$ cells remaining, $+223$ cumulative return). It spends the
most drug ($4.5$ total dose), but places it precisely enough to convert dose
into macrophage repolarisation and tumor clearance, with sustained, targeted
bursts of dosing (Figure~\ref{fig:episode}, bottom). \Sthree{} achieves
partial control ($70$ cells, $+30$ return, $2.8$ dose). It suppresses growth
early but then plateaus, because it lacks the spatial substrate feedback needed
to target the final phase. \Sone{} (POMDP) barely acts ($0.9$ total dose)
and the tumor \emph{grows} past its starting size ($130$ cells, $-6.4$ return).
Seeing only macrophage-polarisation counts, it cannot localise where to inject,
so it under-doses and fails, consistent with its aggregate performance at or
below the random baseline.
